In my third year at Anna University, our college canteen threw away nearly 40 kilograms of food every day. I knew the number because I had spent two weeks weighing the bins myself. That small, slightly embarrassing project became my entry point into demand forecasting, and it is the reason I am applying to the MS in Operations Research at Georgia Tech.

I built a simple model using three semesters of billing data, exam timetables, and weather records. The first version, a linear regression, was wrong on almost every exam day. Students skipped lunch before morning papers and ordered heavily after them, a pattern no single feature captured. Adding an interaction term between exam slot and meal period cut forecast error by 31 percent. The canteen adopted my weekly prep sheet, and waste fell to about 22 kilograms a day over the next term. It was the first time I saw mathematics change something physical.

That experience pushed me to go deeper. I completed NPTEL courses in linear programming and stochastic processes, scoring in the top 5 percent nationally in the latter. For my final-year project under Dr. R. Meenakshi, I formulated a vehicle routing problem for a Chennai milk cooperative with 60 collection points. Our mixed-integer program reduced total route distance by 18 percent in simulation, though it struggled with real-world constraints like drivers refusing certain roads during monsoon flooding. Learning to model that messiness, rather than ignore it, is exactly what I want to study.

After graduating, I joined Zoho as a data analyst on the logistics team for their inventory product. I now maintain forecasting pipelines used by over 200 small retailers. The work has shown me the limits of my training: I can apply standard methods, but I cannot yet design new ones when the standard methods fail.

Georgia Tech's work on stochastic optimization under uncertainty, particularly Professor Alan Erera's research on dynamic routing, speaks directly to the problem I left unsolved with the milk cooperative. I also want to take ISYE 6669 and contribute to the Supply Chain and Logistics Institute's projects with industry partners.

After the MS, I plan to return to India to work on food supply chains, where cold-storage gaps waste nearly a third of perishable produce. I want to build the forecasting and routing systems that small cooperatives cannot afford today. The canteen bins taught me that the problem is real. Georgia Tech can teach me how to solve it at scale.
