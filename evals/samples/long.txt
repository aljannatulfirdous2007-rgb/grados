The first time I saw my grandmother struggle with a hospital form in Madurai, she was holding it upside down. She could read Tamil well, but the form was in English, printed in tiny type, and full of medical terms that even I, a second-year engineering student at the time, had to look up. We spent forty minutes at the registration counter while a queue grew behind us. That afternoon I started wondering why software that could translate an entire web page in a second could not help a seventy-year-old woman fill out a single form. That question has shaped most of what I have built since, and it is why I am applying to the Master of Science in Computer Science at Carnegie Mellon University, with a focus on natural language processing.

My undergraduate years at PSG College of Technology in Coimbatore gave me the foundations. I graduated with a CGPA of 8.7, and my strongest courses were Data Structures, Probability and Statistics, and Machine Learning. But the classroom was where I learned the vocabulary; the real learning happened when I tried to use it. In my third year, I built a prototype called FormSahayak, an Android app that photographed a printed form, extracted the text using OCR, and translated each field into Tamil with a short plain-language explanation. The first version was nearly useless. The OCR confused handwritten entries with printed labels, and the translation model turned "Date of Admission" into a phrase that meant "day you were allowed in," which confused every tester I showed it to.

Fixing those problems taught me more than any course. I learned that general-purpose translation fails on domain-specific language, so I built a small glossary of 400 medical and government form terms with help from two nurses at a local primary health centre. I learned that accuracy on a benchmark means little if the output confuses real users, so I started testing with actual people: my grandmother, her friends at the temple, and patients waiting at the health centre who agreed to try it. By the fourth version, testers could complete a standard hospital registration form in an average of nine minutes instead of twenty-six. The project won second place at the Smart India Hackathon 2024 in the healthcare track, but the moment I remember most is when my grandmother filled out her own pension renewal form without calling me.

For my final-year thesis under Dr. S. Lakshmi Priya, I went deeper into the core problem: low-resource translation for specialised domains. Tamil has plenty of general text online, but very little parallel medical text. I experimented with fine-tuning a multilingual transformer model on a small dataset of 12,000 sentence pairs that I assembled from public health pamphlets, government circulars, and my own glossary. I compared this against a baseline of the same model without fine-tuning and against a commercial translation API. The fine-tuned model improved BLEU scores by 7.4 points over the baseline on medical text, but, more interestingly, human evaluators preferred its output in 68 percent of cases even when BLEU scores were similar. That gap between automatic metrics and human judgement fascinated me, and I wrote the final chapter of my thesis on it. The thesis was later accepted as a short paper at a regional workshop on Indian language technologies.

After graduating, I joined Freshworks in Chennai as a software engineer on the team that builds multilingual support for their customer service product. For the past eighteen months I have worked on the pipeline that detects the language of incoming support tickets and routes them to the right agents. I reduced misrouted tickets for Indian languages by 34 percent by adding a lightweight classifier for code-mixed text, where customers write Tamil or Hindi words in English script, which our previous system consistently misread as English. This work showed me how common code-mixing is in real Indian communication and how poorly most language models handle it. It also showed me the limits of what I can do as an engineer applying existing methods. When the standard approaches fail, I do not yet have the research depth to design better ones. That is the gap I want Carnegie Mellon to close.

I am drawn to CMU specifically because of the Language Technologies Institute's work on low-resource and multilingual NLP. Professor Graham Neubig's research on building language technology for languages with limited data speaks directly to the problem I have been circling for four years. I am also interested in the work on evaluation methods at LTI, since my thesis convinced me that we still measure translation quality badly for the people who need it most. I would like to take the Advanced NLP and Multilingual NLP courses and, if possible, contribute to research on code-mixed language understanding. CMU's emphasis on building systems that are actually used, not only published, matches how I have always worked.

In the long term, I want to return to India and build language tools for the hundreds of millions of people who are shut out of digital services because those services only speak English. Government portals, hospital systems, banking apps, and legal documents all assume a fluency that most Indians do not have. I believe the next decade of Indian technology will be defined by whether we solve this problem, and I want to be one of the people solving it. My immediate goal after the MS is to work at a research lab or company focused on Indian language AI, and eventually to start an organisation that builds open, free language infrastructure for public services.

I still have the upside-down form from that day in Madurai. It reminds me that the hardest problems in technology are rarely about the technology alone. They are about the distance between what a system can do and what a person actually needs. I want to spend my career closing that distance, and I believe Carnegie Mellon is the best place to learn how.
